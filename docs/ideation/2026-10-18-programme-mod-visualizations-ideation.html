Programme Visual Directions

Make product results, decisions, and missing evidence easier to inspect without supervising every agent.

18 October 2026Topic: programme-mod-visualizationsRepository study

Focus: Claude mods for Programme/AutoPM, with Peek as navigation inspiration.

Six directions, not a specification. This document proposes visual relationships and interactions. Nothing here authorizes implementation, approval changes, or publication.

36 raw ideas → 18 distinct concepts + 3 combinations → 21 reviewed candidates → 6 survivors. Fifteen candidates were cut.

Codebase Context

/programme-view reads a saved Programme view and displays flat text. Its builder also exports structured items, decisions, actions, waits, and deliverables.

Established in local sourceNot established
Items, current owners, authored next actions, waits, checker results, references, and check dates.A complete dependency graph, user-behavior acceptance map, or calibrated confidence.

Official mod documentation describes side panes, a prompt band, controls, events, and redraws.UI rendering under claude -p or the Agent SDK. A pane does not establish truthful state.

The existing view omits exact worker claims, rich validation details, historical owners, and earlier answers. Several directions need read access beyond view.json.

The user described repeated questions and supporting investigations that displaced product checks. This is the problem context, not an independently reproduced incident.

Source checks concern the local working checkout. The approval-origin correction remains local and unreleased. No proposed view is installed or runtime-verified.

Shared limits: activity is not progress; a claim is not evidence; an unavailable observation is not a clear result. View generation and evidence collection have different dates.

Public research supplied patterns, not product validation: AgentLens offers layered inspection; SRE separates routine monitoring from actionable exceptions; HAX warns that explanations sometimes increase trust in wrong results.

Topic Axes

All four axes have survivors. The set contains two outcome directions, two coordination directions, one decision direction, and one return direction.

1. Outcome-to-proof inspector

Axis Outcomes and proofConfidence 85%Complexity Medium

Select a stable work item. Unfold its required deliverables into reported completion, checker observations, and missing proof.

Keep unknown, refuted, and confirmed observations distinct. Show the source reference and check date beside each observation. Confirmed deliverables fold into receipts.

A result retains its actual coverage. A build-ancestry check does not prove the user-facing behavior works.

Basis
direct: The sweep states, “A worker’s ‘done’ is a claim, never evidence.” programme_predicate.py:101–108 retains unproven_done. programme_view.py:98–123 exposes deliverables and their observations.
Rationale
One inspection connects a reassuring claim to the observation that supports or contradicts it. The reader does not reconstruct that relationship from activity.
Existing / new
Most item fields exist. Selection, unfolding, exact claim access, and richer validation display are proposals. They are not existing mod behavior.
Downsides
More detail does not guarantee correct checks. An old validate unknown survives some later usable readings. Compare validate.at with checked_at before naming the latest observation.
Discussion test
Does one inspection distinguish an earlier confirmation, a later observation gap, and an old saved view?

2. Changes since the last inspection

Axis Return and changeConfidence 80%Complexity Medium

Return to a comparison, not a transcript. Show changes to evidence, owners, waits, and human questions against a labelled inspection snapshot.

Keep the selected item and reading position stable. Open a changed fact into before-and-after detail, then return to the same place.

Snapshot endpoints show differences, not everything that happened between visits. A missing baseline or incomplete history stays explicit.

Basis
direct: programme_view.py:22,227–244 limits recent activity to eight entries. programme_view.py:293 records generation time. Peek navigation.ts:168–194 restores location on Back.
Rationale
A short activity window does not explain a long absence. Comparing inspected facts reduces repeated orientation without requiring another PM briefing.
Existing / new
Current observations and navigation precedents exist. A per-viewer inspection baseline, comparison logic, and reversal detail are proposals. No durable read-receipt model exists.
Downsides
Baselines need clear scope and lifecycle. An item might change and return to its earlier state between snapshots. History gaps prevent claims of complete coverage.
Discussion test
After more than eight events, does the reader find a changed conclusion and see the comparison’s limits?

3. Scoped decisions with earlier answers

Axis Human decisionsConfidence 80%Complexity Medium

Place a human question beside the relevant earlier answer, its original words, and its named target. Show changed scope instead of presenting a fresh blank question.

Keep related work outside the answer’s boundary unless the recorded scope includes it. Similarity suggests reading material; it never transfers authority.

Opening, selecting, or leaving a question does not answer it. The existing human approval path stays separate.

Basis
direct: Sequencing guidance says, “Do not ask again about an item he has answered.” programme_record.py:502–538 stores questions, choices, and prompt references. programme.py:483–490 retains instruction scope and quoted source words.
Rationale
The user distinguishes forgotten context from genuinely changed scope without rebuilding the earlier conversation.
Existing / new
Questions, answers, and scoped instructions are separate records. Joining them and suggesting related questions are proposals, not a unified authority model.
Downsides
Re-handing replaces the current handed object. Older answers need journal or history access. Similar wording does not establish equivalent scope; ambiguous matches remain labelled.
Discussion test
Does the reader understand why a new answer is necessary without accidentally extending an earlier answer?

4. Why is this a gate?

Axis Work coordinationConfidence 60%Complexity High

Select a wait or supporting investigation. Inspect the required product check it serves, the recorded dependency reason, the restriction source, and the stopping condition.

Show an absent connection as an absent connection. Do not infer that supporting work is necessary, irrelevant, or wasteful from the layout.

Separate a human boundary, a runtime refusal, a PM assignment choice, and a worker assumption when source records establish that distinction.

Basis
direct: The user said the PM does not stop to “assess or validate its own requirements.” programme_record.py:598–636 stores optional item links and queue reasons. Sequencing guidance explicitly says dependency gates are not built.
Rationale
Making a blocking assumption inspectable addresses process drift before supporting work displaces the required product check.
Existing / new
Authored actions, some item links, waits, and instruction scope exist. A complete gate-source, dependency-reason, or stopping-condition model does not. Those associations are proposed data.
Downsides
The view risks creating more reporting work. Start from available records and expose gaps; do not require every action to acquire a justification record. The reported Linux and source-only incidents were not independently replayed.
Confidence limit
The independent critique marked the basis weak because its supplied sources lacked the concrete anecdotes and complete link model. The direction remains because the user explicitly identified the problem. No runtime or data-model claim follows from that report.
Discussion test
Does inspection reveal an unestablished acceptance dependency without declaring that the investigation has no value?

5. Who has the next move?

Axis Work coordinationConfidence 80%Complexity Medium

Show a wait through its separate roles: the party producing the result, the current item owner, and the watcher or reporter noticing the result.

Attach the next recorded action. Leave missing responsibility or movement conditions visibly empty rather than inferring a complete chain.

Keep work-item identity stable when workers change. Inspection does not dispatch work or prompt an old pane.

Basis
direct: programme_record.py:389–401 records who, watcher, and reporter. programme_predicate.py:78–87 treats a named reporter as coverage without proving a live observation.
Rationale
The reader distinguishes an owned wait from an abandoned next move without checking every active worker.
Existing / new
Current ownership and wait roles exist. The joined visual chain, historical ownership display, and navigation are proposals. A general movement-condition field does not exist.
Downsides
Recorded coverage might be old. A heartbeat does not establish success. Do not turn adjacent roles into dependency edges or treat one reporter as a shared cause.
Discussion test
Does the reader identify who produces the result, who observes it, and which role is missing?

6. What can the user do now?

Axis Outcomes and proofConfidence 60%Complexity High

Organize work around a user-visible product moment instead of a worker or task. Show what a user now does, its supporting observations, and unobserved behavior.

Unfold that moment into related work and required checks. Keep merged code, deployment, checker confirmation, and observed product behavior distinct.

This is the most product-focused direction. It also needs the largest new semantic connection between work and acceptance.

Basis
direct: The user wants product checks rather than supporting activity. Existing item observations appear in programme_view.py:98–123. The observed-effects learning states, “Count observed effects, or say unknown.”
Rationale
A task board answers what moved. This lens asks whether the product became more useful and exposes where that claim has no observation.
Existing / new
Item deliverables exist. Product-moment grouping, user-behavior acceptance links, and inferred associations are proposals. Inferred links need clear labels and source inspection.
Downsides
Generated product stories risk sounding persuasive without evidence. Mapping work to behavior costs effort and risks another reporting ritual. Do not invent completed acceptance from a deployment receipt.
Discussion test
Does the reader name one newly possible user action and identify exactly what nobody has observed?

Rejection Summary

The critique weighed source support, expected value, novelty, burden, reuse, and overlap. Meeting-level value mattered more than feasible presentation.

The independent review found 16 sound bases and five weak bases. Thirteen candidates passed its discussion floor. No failed-floor candidate was promoted.

How the 36 raw ideas were combined into 18 concepts
  • User-visible product moments: “Show me what the user can do now.”
  • Scoped decisions with earlier answers: “A question arrives with its earlier answer”; “A precedent with its scope cut out”; “A jurisdiction envelope around every answer”; “A revised-question docket”; “Make scope the frame around the question.”
  • Changes since the last inspection: “Resume the inspection, not the transcript”; “Return to the difference, not the history”; “Resume at the broken assumption”; “The restoration overlay”; “Return to a changed scene”; “Design for the pane being closed.”
  • Next-move responsibility chains: “Who has the next move?”; “Show the empty chair”; “The relay exchange zone”; “Show the next hand, not the busiest worker.”
  • Claim beside checked evidence: “Put the claim beside the thing that could disprove it”; “Put the claim beside its witness.”
  • Coordination tied to its intended result: “What is all this coordination buying?”
  • Missing-proof inspector: “Make missing proof the main view”; “The assurance stencil”; “Read the programme backwards from the result.”
  • One question with its context: “Put the question on trial, not the whole programme”; “A reading room for one consequential question.”
  • Automatically staged evidence inspector: “Let the proof arrive at your seat.”
  • Quiet exception line: “A quiet pane must explain its quiet”; “A programme that folds to one line.”
  • Reuse verification routes, not verdicts: “Reuse the proof path, not the green result.”
  • Stable items and owner handoff history: “Pass the unresolved test with the work”; “Keep an item in view when its worker disappears.”
  • Recurring gaps in observation: “Find the recurring hole in the evidence.”
  • History of overturned conclusions: “Keep the moment the plan changed.”
  • Result and observation health separated: “A control sample beside the result.”
  • Intention beside observed change: “Give the PM two facing pages.”
  • Shared waiting rooms: “Hundreds of items, one shared waiting room.”
  • Dated view and separate evidence clocks: “Let the pane admit it is a photograph.”

Duplicates were combined because they address the same decision through different presentation metaphors. Each resulting concept received critique. Three additional combinations were also reviewed: the proof inspector, gate inquiry, and comparison sheet.
